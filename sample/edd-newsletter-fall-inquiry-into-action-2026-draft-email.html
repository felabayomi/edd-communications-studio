EdD Program Update

A letter to our learning community

Inquiry into action

Program leadership · demonstration message

The questions that matter most rarely stay inside a classroom. They emerge in staff meetings, community conversations, learning spaces, and the moments when familiar approaches stop working. This season, we invite our EdD community to bring those questions into conversation with one another.

Our theme is simple: listen closely, investigate thoughtfully, and share what you learn. A useful next step might be a more inclusive meeting, a sharper research question, or a new partnership shaped with—not merely for—the people it serves.

In this edition, we explore the connections between reflective practice, belonging, and public scholarship. Wherever you are in your doctoral journey, make room for a colleague’s perspective and for the possibility that your next insight will begin with a better question.

What changes when we treat the people closest to a challenge as partners in understanding it?

EdD Community

Community practice

Connection is part of the work

Doctoral work can be solitary; it does not have to be isolating. Try a small peer check-in: share one idea that is taking shape, one obstacle, and one specific kind of feedback you would welcome. Listening before advising makes room for different contexts and different ways of knowing.

For your next conversation, bring an artifact of practice—a meeting agenda, a learning activity, or a question from a community partner—and ask what it reveals about participation and voice.

A strong learning community makes it easier to ask an unfinished question.

Try this with a peer

The five-minute reading exchange

Choose one passage from a reading that challenged an assumption. In a brief exchange, explain why it stayed with you and ask your partner what it brings to mind in their own setting. Resist the urge to agree too quickly: a useful conversation can reveal where the same idea meets different realities.

What does this idea help us see—and what might it leave out?

Dissertation Journey

The dissertation journey

From a compelling concern to a researchable question

A problem of practice begins with a concern you can describe clearly in context. Before narrowing a question, write a short account of who experiences the problem, what you currently know, and what remains uncertain.

Then consider the fit: what evidence would help answer the question, who should shape the inquiry, and what is feasible within your setting? Bring that working account to your next advising conversation. The goal is not a perfect first formulation; it is a defensible next step.

Residency

Preparing for residency

Arrive with a question. Leave with a connection.

Residency creates an opportunity to connect sustained inquiry with shared conversation. Prepare a two-minute introduction to your current work, choose an artifact that illustrates your practice, and identify a question that would benefit from perspectives beyond your own field.

Build in time to listen, reflect, and follow up. Afterward, send one colleague a resource or question that continues the conversation. Confirm all schedules, locations, and preparation requirements through official program communications.

Making room for every voice

An educator’s inquiry into belonging

In this fictional composite story, a school-based educator notices that the same few voices tend to shape professional learning meetings. Rather than assuming that silence means agreement, the practitioner begins asking how the meeting itself might influence participation.

Their developing inquiry considers reflective writing, smaller discussion groups, and opportunities to contribute before and after a meeting. The emphasis is on listening to colleagues and examining the conditions that help people participate.

The story illustrates the kind of practice-centered student feature this newsletter could carry. It does not describe a real Antioch student, study, or research finding.

I began by asking how to improve participation. I am learning to ask who gets to define what participation looks like.

Carrying inquiry beyond the dissertation

A continuing practice of learning with others

In this fictional alumni profile, a community-organization leader carries a reflective inquiry habit into everyday work. The team sets aside time to examine a service experience from the perspective of the people who use it, then invites those participants to help shape the next questions.

The most valuable change is not a new title or a finished report. It is a more deliberate routine: notice assumptions, seek perspectives that are missing, and make decisions that can be revisited as understanding grows.

Research & Public Scholarship

Research & public scholarship

Scholarship that travels beyond the seminar

Who could use your ideas beyond an academic audience? Consider a short practice brief, a facilitated conversation, or a visual explanation that helps a community partner engage with a question. Begin with what the audience needs to understand, and make the limits of the evidence as clear as the possibilities.

Environmental learning offers one useful lens: questions about place, responsibility, and participation can bring professional practice into conversation with community priorities. The photograph is illustrative brand imagery, not documentation of an EdD research project.

Opportunities

An invitation to share your thinking

Turn one idea into a practice brief

Choose a question from your professional setting and draft a one-page brief for a nonacademic audience. Describe the context, explain why the question matters, and offer one carefully qualified implication for practice. Ask a colleague outside your specialization to read it for clarity.

This is an editorial activity for the mockup, not a funded opportunity, formal call for proposals, or required assignment.

Professional learning

Find the conversation your work belongs in

As you consider workshops, conferences, and community forums, look for audiences connected to your problem of practice. Review the purpose, accessibility, cost, and submission requirements before committing. A small local conversation can be as useful as a large conference when the right partners are at the table.

Resources & Support

Writing & research support

Make space for the next draft

When writing feels stalled, separate the tasks. Use one session to clarify the claim, another to organize evidence, and a third to revise for a reader. Bring a focused question when seeking feedback: “Where does the argument become difficult to follow?” is often more useful than “Is this good?”
